You can’t reduce what you don’t measure.

AI work is usually judged on accuracy and speed, rarely on energy, so its real cost stays hidden. Measuring shows where the energy goes, so we can act on it: choose the models, hardware and settings that do the same work with less, and check that a change really saved energy.

What we measure, and how

We measure different kinds of machines side by side: a desktop AI computer with an Arm processor (DGX Spark), two Intel workstations with NVIDIA graphics cards, and a small edge AI board (Jetson).

1

At the plug

A smart plug between the socket and each machine measures its total power: the number the electricity bill is based on.

2

Inside the machine

The processor and the graphics card report their own power. A small program on each machine reads them every 5 seconds.

3

Put together

We collect every reading, keep all of it, and turn energy into cost (₹8 per kWh) and CO₂ (0.675 kg per kWh, the national grid average).

The gap between the plug and the chips is the rest of the machine: memory, motherboard, fans, disks and power-supply losses.

Good practices for GreenAI experiments

How to measure the energy of an AI experiment properly, and how to serve language models with less energy, from what we learned running this lab and our own GPU runs.

01Setting up

Measure at the plug, not only the chip.

Processor and GPU counters (RAPL, NVML) leave out memory, storage, fans and power-supply losses. Say which parts your tools cover, and don’t call a CPU-plus-GPU total whole-machine energy.

  • In our labunder load the DGX Spark’s GPU reports about 40 W while its plug reads about 125 W.
  • SustAInd · SWEnergy (AGENT 2026)RAPL and NVML report only processor and graphics energy, so their totals are a partial cost, not what the machine draws from the wall.

Record the idle baseline and subtract it.

Measure each machine with your workload stopped, then remove that draw from every trace you report.

  • In our labthe DGX Spark draws about 25 W idle and about 123 W under load.

Count only your own work.

Run on an otherwise idle machine where you can, and track your workload by its process ID so other software isn’t counted against you.

  • In our labseveral people run experiments on the same machines, sometimes at the same time.
  • SustAInd · Watts and Debts (ESEM 2026)the study follows the model server by its process ID and subtracts its energy from the total to get the framework’s own cost.

Measure what your monitoring itself costs.

Don’t start a new process (such as nvidia-smi) for every sample.

  • In our labour reader uses under 0.1% of one CPU core. One GPU reading cost 3–7 ms of CPU per read, about 100 times the rest, so we stopped reading it.

02While it runs

Warm up, repeat, then average.

Discard warm-up runs and compare the average of several runs, not a single reading.

Record temperature and power limits.

A GPU held back by its power limit or by heat runs slower, which changes energy per task.

  • In our labduring one experiment the A2000’s GPU sat at its 70 W limit at 85–86 °C, held back by the power limit the whole time.

Where you can’t read energy counters, use stand-ins.

On many virtual machines and cloud servers, runtime and output tokens are good proxies for energy.

  • SustAInd · SWEnergy (AGENT 2026)energy closely tracked wall-clock time (R = 0.89) and output tokens (R = 0.88), while memory use showed almost no link.

03Comparing and reporting

Compare on the same machine with the same tool, and check across machines.

Absolute energy figures don’t carry over between setups, so claim only the effects that hold on every machine you test.

  • In our labwe run Arm and Intel machines side by side, so the same experiment can be checked on both.

Report energy per completed task, next to accuracy and time.

A cheap run may simply have failed or given up early.

Write down the setup.

Hardware, driver version, model, quantization, batch size, power limit, sampling interval, and the tariff and CO₂ factor you used.

04Serving language models efficiently

These save energy when you run models for real work. In an energy experiment each one is also a setting that changes what you measure, so choose it on purpose, keep it the same across the runs you compare, and report it.

Batch requests when serving.

A GPU decoding several requests together gets much more done in the same time, which lowers the energy per token.

The trade-off:when you measure energy per task, the GPU’s power can’t be split exactly between requests running together, so run them one at a time or report the batch size.

  • In our runstwo requests at once gave 1.4–1.9 times the throughput of one.

Use lower precision where quality allows.

8-bit (FP8) models need less memory and often run on one GPU instead of two.

The trade-off:lower precision can cost accuracy, and a model that writes longer answers can use more energy per task, so measure energy and accuracy together and report the precision you used.

Shut down servers you aren’t using.

A GPU with a model loaded keeps drawing power while it waits.

For more, and the papers behind many of these points, see SustAInd: Best Practices.
